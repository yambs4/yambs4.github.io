Using Propensity Score Matching to Evaluate PSNP Capacity Development Impact

How a quasi-experimental design can help assess program capacity when randomization is not feasible.

The Productive Safety Net Programme is one of Ethiopia’s most important social protection programs. Its capacity development investments are intended to strengthen the institutions and systems that deliver the program. Evaluating those investments requires more than describing activities: it requires a credible comparison of what happened with the intervention and what might have happened without it.

The evaluation question

The central question was whether PSNP IV capacity development initiatives improved government implementation capacity, beneficiary targeting, and the timely delivery of public works. Because assignment was not randomized, the evaluation used observational data and a quasi-experimental strategy.

Why propensity score matching?

Propensity Score Matching (PSM) estimates the probability that an observation receives treatment based on pre-treatment characteristics. Treated and comparison observations with similar estimated probabilities can then be compared, reducing observable differences between the groups.

  • Estimate propensity scores using pre-treatment covariates.
  • Match treated and comparison woredas using nearest-neighbor matching.
  • Check covariate balance using standardized mean differences.
  • Estimate the average treatment effect on the treated.

A compact R workflow

library(MatchIt); ps_model <- glm(treatment ~ covariate1 + covariate2, data = df, family = binomial); matched <- matchit(ps_model, method = "nearest", ratio = 1)

What the results suggest

The evaluation results indicated a 34% improvement in government implementation capacity, an 18% improvement in beneficiary targeting accuracy, and a 25% reduction in timely public works delivery delays.

These findings should be read alongside the design’s assumptions: matching can address differences captured by the observed covariates, but it cannot remove bias from unobserved factors. That is why balance checks, transparent reporting, and sensitivity analysis matter as much as the treatment-effect estimate itself.
